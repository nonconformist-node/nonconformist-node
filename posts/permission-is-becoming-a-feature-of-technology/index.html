Permission Is Becoming a Feature of Technology

You don’t own most of the technology you use today.

You might have the phone in your hand. The computer on your desk. You might even have paid for the software running on it.

But having something is not the same as owning it.

Your computer may require identity verification. Your files may be on the cloud – someone else’s computer. Most smart home and security systems depend on servers you don’t control. And the conversations you have with your favourite AI may never touch your hardware.

This road was taken willingly.

Convenience won. Maintenance, responsibility and technical complexity were abstracted away in exchange for simplicity. It seemed like a good trade – until the shackles became clear.

The odd part is that our technology has become vastly more capable while our control over it has moved in the opposite direction.

A Strange Reversal

The computer used to be a machine you controlled. Now, it is becoming a machine you access.

The early internet was a network where almost anyone could run a server, publish a website or create a new protocol. Today, much of our experience of the internet happens inside platforms that decide what can be published, who can participate and which rules apply.

AI is taking this even further. We have machines with extraordinary capabilities, yet most of that intelligence is offered as a service. You don’t run the model. You rent access to it.

Even hardware is following the same trajectory. Devices are becoming more tightly controlled, harder to modify and sometimes deliberately difficult to repair.

Old model: Control → responsibility

Current model: Access → dependency

What happens when the technologies that give us more power also give someone else more power to decide whether we’re allowed to use it?

The Trade

In essence, there is nothing wrong with convenience.

Accounts and cloud services are useful. Platforms need some moderation. The problem is that convenience can hide dependency.

When someone else takes responsibility for maintaining the system, they also acquire control over the system. When that control becomes invisible, we can mistake access for ownership. Unaware of the trade-offs, most users give up their agency.

Agency is the ability to act without permission, to choose your own direction without waiting for someone else to approve the next step.

Technology can give us thousands of choices while quietly narrowing the boundaries within which those choices can be made.

You can publish anything you want.
Except the platform can remove it.

You can run whatever software you want.
Except the software may require a subscription or server authorization.

You can access powerful intelligence.
But not under your control.

You can send money.
Except an intermediary can freeze the account or reject the transaction.

You can participate online.
Except you need an account, phone number, identity verification, or platform credentials.

The Hacker Instinct

The hacker instinct is to look at those boundaries and ask:

Why does it have to work this way?

The answer isn’t to reject technology, or worse, ask for regulation. It’s to gain awareness and build alternatives where control can be moved closer to the person actually using the technology.

Open-source software gives you control over the software itself. You can inspect it, modify it and keep using it without depending entirely on the vendor’s permission.

Peer-to-peer networks move control over communication away from a single intermediary. The network becomes something participants operate rather than merely something they access.

Self-hosting moves data and infrastructure back onto hardware you control. A small computer running your own services can replace an entire layer of dependency on someone else’s cloud.

Open hardware applies the same principle to the physical world. Devices designed around repairability, open standards and accessible firmware give users more control over what their machines can become.

Cryptography can make privacy a property of the system rather than a promise made by an intermediary. Instead of asking someone to protect a conversation for you, mathematics can make unauthorized access considerably harder.

Local AI is starting to reduce reliance on centralized gated intelligence.

None of these alternatives are frictionless. They require knowledge, maintenance and sometimes compromise.

That is precisely the point.

Convenience gained by giving up responsibility comes with a loss of control. Taking some responsibility back is the price of regaining it.

Capability Isn’t Autonomy

Cypherpunks understood that asking institutions to respect your freedom gets limited results. Build tools that constrain their power to take it away.

A system doesn’t become free simply because its operator promises to behave well. There is a fundamental difference between trusting someone not to take your freedom away and using a system that doesn’t give them the technical ability to do so in the first place.

Technology can make us extraordinarily capable.

But capability and autonomy aren’t the same thing.

The tools are available. Will you use them, or let someone else decide for you?

If you still choose permissioned technology, just understand the trade-off: convenience in exchange of control.

The most interesting technologies are those that don’t just give us more power – but give us more control over whether that power can be taken away.
